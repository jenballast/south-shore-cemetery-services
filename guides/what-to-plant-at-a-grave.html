What to plant at a grave site

The hardiness zone is the easy part. The harder constraint is a planting that still looks cared for six weeks after anyone was there.

A planting at a grave site has to do something no garden bed has to do: look cared for when nobody has been there for six weeks. That constraint, more than anything about climate, is what should drive the plant list.

What zone 6b to 7a means here

The South Shore sits in USDA hardiness zones 6b and 7a. Under the 2023 revision of the USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map, the coastal towns — Hull, Scituate, Cohasset, Marshfield, Duxbury, Plymouth, Hingham — largely fall in 7a, with 6b appearing as you move inland and north through towns like Quincy, Weymouth, and Kingston.

Practically, the number is an average annual minimum winter temperature: roughly -5°F to 0°F for 6b, and 0°F to 5°F for 7a. It tells you whether a perennial will survive the winter. It tells you nothing about summer heat, drought, wind, or salt, all of which matter more at an exposed cemetery plot than the winter low does.

The ocean is why the coastal strip runs warmer — water moderates the winter minimums. It is also why those same plots deal with wind and salt spray that an inland plot never sees. A plant rated for your zone can still fail at a coastal grave site for reasons the zone map does not capture.

The constraint that actually governs: root systems and space

Most cemeteries limit plantings to a defined area around the stone, and the limit is often tighter than families expect. High Street Cemetery, where we care for one of our subscription plots, permits annual plantings within twelve inches abutting the stone. Twelve inches is not much bed, and it rules out anything that spreads.

Rules vary considerably from one cemetery to the next — some allow a wider border, some permit only planters and no in-ground planting at all, and some prohibit plantings entirely and allow cut flowers only. This is worth confirming before you buy anything, because a planting that violates the rules gets removed, usually without notice.

Within whatever space you have, the plants that work share a profile: compact and well-behaved at the root, low enough not to obscure the inscription, tolerant of drought and reflected heat off the stone, and forgiving of neglect between visits. Anything that runs, self-seeds aggressively, or needs deadheading to keep looking presentable is the wrong choice — not because it is a bad plant, but because nobody is going to be there to manage it.

What we plant, by season

These are the varieties we use across South Shore sites, chosen for those conditions:

  • Spring — tulips, daffodils, and pansies for early color. Daffodils are the standout for a grave site: they naturalize, return reliably year after year, and deer and rodents leave them alone, which is not true of tulips.
  • Summer — marigolds, zinnias, and vinca, all heat- and drought-tolerant and blooming right through the season. Where a permanent, tidy planting is preferred, low sedum or dianthus instead.
  • Fall — chrysanthemums, the classic flower of remembrance, in the deep reds, golds, and purples of the season.
  • Heavy shade — hosta, coral bells, or daylilies, which handle low light and are undemanding once established.

For winter, a Christmas wreath is the more usual choice than a planting, and it comes off on schedule rather than being left to weather through to spring.

Perennials or annuals

Both have a real case, and the right answer depends less on the plants than on how often anyone will be there.

Perennials — sedum, dianthus, daylilies, hosta, naturalized daffodils — cost less over time, need no replanting, and hold the space year-round so nothing looks bare in the off season. The trade-off is a narrower window of bloom and the need for occasional dividing and cutting back. A perennial planting left entirely alone for several years does not stay tidy; it gets rangy, and at a cemetery that reads as neglect rather than as a garden.

Annuals give a longer and more reliable run of color and let the site change with the season, which for a lot of families is the entire point — the fall mums are a marker that someone came. They cost more each year and require someone to plant and to clear them.

In practice most of the sites we care for use a low perennial base for structure with seasonal annuals rotated at the front, which keeps the plot from looking empty in February without giving up the seasonal changes. Note that this is not an option everywhere — some cemeteries permit annuals only. High Street Cemetery in Hingham prohibits perennials outright, which is exactly the kind of rule worth checking before you buy anything.

Practical notes

  • Do not plant hard against the stone. Foliage held against granite traps moisture and encourages the biological growth that cleaning is meant to remove — one reason a well-intentioned planting can shorten the interval between cleanings.
  • Avoid anything with a woody or aggressive root system near the foundation. Shrubs and small trees are prohibited at most cemeteries for exactly this reason.
  • Ivy is best avoided even where allowed. It holds moisture against stone, works into joints, and is genuinely difficult to remove once established.
  • Mulch lightly and keep it off the stone base — but check first, as some cemeteries prohibit bed coverings entirely. St. Mary's in Scituate allows neither mulch nor decorative stone.
  • A plot in full sun with no irrigation is a harder site than most people plan for. Err toward drought tolerance.

Check the rules before you plant

To repeat the point because it is the one that costs people money: confirm the cemetery's regulations before planting anything. Ask about the permitted planting area, whether in-ground planting is allowed at all, restrictions on species or height, and the dates when seasonal décor and plantings are cleared. Most South Shore cemeteries are town-administered, so the Town Clerk, Cemetery Department, or DPW is where to ask.

Working out those rules for each site is part of what our annual care plans handle — every planting we place follows the cemetery's own regulations, with seasonal décor removed on schedule, so nothing gets pulled unexpectedly.
