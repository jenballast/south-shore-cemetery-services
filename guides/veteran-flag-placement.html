Flag placement and grave care for veterans

What actually happens at a veteran's grave on Memorial Day and Veterans Day, and how to prepare the site ahead of time.

Flags at veterans' graves are one of the more visible traditions in a South Shore cemetery every May and November, and one families sometimes lose track of once nobody local is checking on the site regularly. Here's how it actually works, and what we do for the veterans' plots we care for.

Who places the flags, and when

In most South Shore towns, small American flags at veterans' graves go in ahead of Memorial Day, typically placed in bulk by veterans' organizations, Boy Scout and Girl Scout troops, or town Veterans' Agents in the days before the last Monday in May. Veterans Day, on November 11th, sometimes sees a second smaller round of placement or flag replacement in towns that hold graveside ceremonies, though it's less universal than the Memorial Day tradition. Either way, if your family plot includes a veteran, a flag placed at the base of the stone is standard practice, not something you need to arrange privately in most cases.

Where families come in is when a specific individual placement matters to them — a flag placed by hand on the actual day, rather than left to whatever bulk placement the town does — or when the grave is in a small or older section that town crews sometimes miss.

What we do at veterans' graves

If your family's plot includes a veteran, we'll place a flag for Memorial Day or Veterans Day at no charge — just let us know when you book any other service, or reach out specifically for this. It takes a minute, and it's the right thing to do while we're already standing there. We also remove and properly retire flags once they've weathered past a dignified condition, rather than leaving a faded or torn flag in place through the following season.

Several cemeteries we work in hold real concentrations of veterans' graves — Kingston's Old Burying Ground has 22 local veterans of the Revolution, Rockland's Mount Pleasant Cemetery holds the G.A.R. Soldiers' Lot and the 1913 monument to the Unreturned Soldiers, and Hull Village Cemetery sees local soldiers' graves decorated every Memorial Day. In these plots especially, flag condition and stone condition tend to go together — a family stone that hasn't been cleaned in years is often in a section where nobody's tending to much else either.

Cleaning the stone before the holiday

Flags look best against a legible, cared-for stone rather than one dulled by lichen or algae. If you want the site looking its best for Memorial Day, the timing matters: a proper cleaning takes about three visits over several weeks, so it needs to start well ahead of the holiday, not the week before. As a rule of thumb, beginning in March gives enough time for a full cleaning to be finished before Memorial Day weekend. Starting in early May is usually too late for the stone to look fully restored by the day itself, though a single tidy-up visit can still make a real difference.

If you're not sure a flag has been placed

This comes up more than you'd think, especially for families who live too far away to check in person. If you'd like us to confirm a flag is in place, place one ourselves, or send a photo either way, just tell us the cemetery and roughly where the plot is. It's a small thing to check, and for a family who can't be there, knowing it's been done matters.

How the rules differ town by town

We want to be straight about the limits of what we can tell you here. Flag policy on the South Shore is set at the cemetery and town level rather than centrally, and it is not published consistently — some towns post their cemetery regulations, many do not, and the practice at a given burying ground is often governed by long-standing local custom rather than a written rule. Rather than list specifics we cannot stand behind, here is what genuinely varies, and who to ask.

The things that differ from one town or cemetery to the next:

  • Who may place flags. Some cemeteries handle veterans' flags exclusively through the town Veterans' Services Officer and the veterans' organizations, and ask that families not place their own. Others have no restriction at all.
  • Holder type. Where flags go in permanent bronze or metal holders installed at the grave, cemeteries often specify the holder and prohibit families from installing their own. Where placement is seasonal, a simple staff pressed into the ground is the norm.
  • Removal deadlines. Cemeteries that clear seasonal décor on a schedule generally clear flags on the same schedule. The date varies, and a flag left past it is removed rather than left.
  • Whether Veterans Day placement happens at all. The Memorial Day tradition is close to universal here. A November round is not, and depends on whether the town holds a graveside observance.

The reliable way to get an answer for a specific grave is to call the town Veterans' Services Officer (sometimes still titled Veterans' Agent) in the town where the cemetery is. That office coordinates the placement and will know both the written rule and the actual practice. For questions about the cemetery's own regulations — décor, removal dates, holders — the Town Clerk, Cemetery Department, or DPW is the right call, depending on how the town is organized. If the cemetery is a private or church burying ground rather than a municipal one, its own trustees set the rules and the town cannot answer for them.

Where a cemetery does publish its rule, it is worth reading. St. Mary's Cemetery in Scituate, for example, sets an explicit flag season of May 1 through November 30 and collects flags left up past that date, turning them over to the Veterans' Administration — a clearer and firmer rule than most. We note published rules like that on our individual cemetery pages as we confirm them.

If you tell us which cemetery, we are usually happy to make that call on your behalf — it is a short conversation and we make it often.

General flag etiquette at a grave

Where no local rule says otherwise, standard practice is straightforward. The flag goes at the base of the headstone, centered, upright, and set deep enough that wind will not take it over. Placement happens in the days before the holiday rather than on the morning of it, which is how the bulk placements are timed.

A flag left in place should not be left indefinitely. Once it has faded, frayed, or torn, the respectful course is to retire it and replace it rather than let it weather in place — worn flags are properly disposed of by burning, and local VFW and American Legion posts accept them for that purpose. This is the part families most often lose track of from a distance, and it is one of the things we handle at the veterans' plots we care for.

One practical note: a flag assumes you know where the grave is. If you are trying to place one at an ancestor's grave and have not yet located it, how to find an ancestor's grave on the South Shore covers working that out.
