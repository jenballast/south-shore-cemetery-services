Cohasset Central Cemetery

Three centuries of stone above Little Harbor, on the National Register since 2002 — and closed to visitors four months of the year.

Cohasset Central Cemetery sits at Joy Place, off North Main Street, on ground overlooking Little Harbor. Roughly 500 gravestones across 4.5 acres, with burials running from 1705 to the present — it is the most historically significant burying ground we work in, and it was added to the National Register of Historic Places on December 27, 2002.

It is neither town-run nor parish-run. The cemetery is privately owned by its lot owners, with a Board of Directors responsible for maintenance and preservation. That makes it a third governance model among the cemeteries we work in, and it means questions about lots, records, and permissions go to the board at info@cohassetcentralcemetery.com rather than to the Town of Cohasset.

Access is seasonal. The cemetery is open 7:00 a.m. to sunset from April 1 through November 30, and closed from December 1 through March 31. Plan any winter visit — or winter work — around that. It is one reason we schedule Cohasset Central plots for a spring start rather than a late-season one.

How the ground came together

The cemetery began as a colonial burying ground on common town land. It grew by donation: Samuel Bates deeded acreage to the south in 1825, and Edward Tower added land to the north in 1867. The oldest section is still known as the Old Slates, and it holds the graves of Cohasset's earliest families — Towers, Pratts, Lincolns, Bates, Beals, and Hobarts. Farmers and fishermen, shipbuilders and seafarers.

The first burial is recorded in 1705, that of Margaret Tower. The oldest surviving stone is believed to be Sarah Pratt's, dated 1706 and carved with a death's head by the First North River carver.

Later additions reflect the cemetery's continued use: a brick receiving shed built in 1873 that now serves as the caretaker's building, cast-iron fencing in a twig-and-branch pattern, columbaria named for Prescott T. Cumner (1982), Edward E. Tower (1988), and Daniel C. Cotton (2007), and a 500-foot stone seawall completed in 2019.

The carvers, and why they matter for cleaning

This is the part that makes Cohasset Central different from almost anywhere else we work. The Old Slates section holds documented eighteenth-century work by the Scituate school of carvers — the First and Second North River Carvers, active roughly 1700 to 1778, and Jacob Vinal Sr. and Jr., roughly 1715 to 1781.

Their designs track a genuine shift in belief. The earliest stones carry skulls, crossbones, and winged death's heads, in the Puritan idiom of memento mori. Later stones move to winged cherubim and angels, reflecting the Great Awakening. The nineteenth century brings marble and granite in urns, obelisks, scrolls, and statuary.

Practically, this means a single cemetery holding thin colonial slate, Victorian marble, and modern granite within a few hundred feet of each other — three materials that want three different approaches. Granite, marble, and slate covers how each weathers and what each can safely take. The eighteenth-century slates in particular are folk art of real documented value, and they are the last stones anyone should approach with a brush and a household cleaner — why D/2 and not bleach explains what is at stake.

The Brig St. John

On October 7, 1849, the brig St. John, carrying Irish emigrants toward Boston, was driven onto the Grampuses — low rocks off Cohasset — in a storm after her anchors failed. Of 143 people aboard, 20 survived: nine crew and eleven passengers. Only forty-five bodies were ever recovered from the water.

Those forty-five were buried here in a common trench grave, which remains unmarked. In 1915 the Ancient Order of Hibernians erected a large stone Celtic cross in their memory. The cross stands elsewhere in the cemetery rather than at the grave itself — worth knowing if you come looking for the burial site specifically.

For families researching Irish famine-era emigration, this is one of the more significant sites on the South Shore, and the cemetery's own account of the wreck is well worth reading.

Other notable burials

  • Rev. Nehemiah Hobart, the parish's first pastor, died 1740.
  • Levi B. Gaylord, a Congressional Medal of Honor recipient.
  • Abraham Tower and James Stoddard, both participants in the Boston Tea Party.
  • Generations of Cohasset sea captains — James Hall, George Hall, and Thomas Stoddard among them.

The cemetery maintains a walking tour of 64 marked points covering these and other stones, which is the best introduction to the grounds if you can visit in person.

Finding a burial here

Cohasset Central is better documented than most cemeteries on the South Shore. The board publishes a complete burial database as a searchable spreadsheet, alphabetical by surname, covering every person interred here. Each entry carries the plot or columbarium number, date of death, plot ownership, and book and page references. Burials in the Old Slates sections 1 through 6 are annotated with plain-language locations — "north of the brick shed," "between plots 370–372" — because those early sections do not follow the later numbering.

The site also publishes plot and columbarium plans that correspond to the numbers in the database, so in most cases you can go from a surname to a location on a map without contacting anyone. That is unusual, and it makes this a good cemetery to research from a distance. How to find an ancestor's grave on the South Shore covers the wider search when a name does not turn up.

If you have a plot number but cannot get to Cohasset, we can walk the grounds, locate the stone, and photograph it for a one-time fee of $15–$25 — useful here in particular, since an eighteenth-century slate that reads as blank in ordinary light is often still legible in raking light or after a careful cleaning.

Plantings and decoration rules

We have not found published regulations on plantings, decorations, or seasonal removal for Cohasset Central, and we are not going to guess at them. Given the cemetery's historic status and the age of the Old Slates section, we would expect the board to be protective about anything placed near the early stones, but that is an expectation rather than a rule we can point to.

Contact the board at info@cohassetcentralcemetery.com before planting or placing anything. If you learn the current rules, we would be glad to hear them — we will add them here with a note on when they were confirmed.

For general guidance on what suits a grave site in this climate, see what to plant at a grave site. Note that rules differ sharply from one cemetery to the next — High Street in Hingham permits annuals only, while St. Mary's in Scituate allows perennials and prohibits mulch.

Stone care here

The setting that makes this cemetery beautiful is hard on the stone. Ground overlooking Little Harbor means direct salt exposure, and salt is what accelerates surface erosion and gives lichen and algae a better grip. Coastal plots here green up noticeably faster than inland ones a few miles west, and generally want attention every 12 to 18 months rather than every two years — why coastal headstones need more frequent cleaning covers the mechanism.

Combine that with three centuries of different materials and the answer is never one method. We clean with D/2 Biological Solution across a minimum of three visits, with no pressure washing, no wire brushes, and nothing abrasive on any stone here.
