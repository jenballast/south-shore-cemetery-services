Milton Cemetery

The town's only cemetery since 1672, on the National Register — and an accredited arboretum whose canopy is the main thing working against its stones.

Milton Cemetery is at 211 Centre Street, at the corner of Randolph Avenue, and it is the Town of Milton's only cemetery. It was established in 1672, ten years after the town was incorporated, and has grown across more than 330 years to its present 102 acres. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places on 2 June 2004.

It is town-run, managed by the Milton Cemetery Department under a board of Trustees of the Cemetery. Lisa Ahern is Cemetery Director. The office is in the converted stable beside the white Victorian house at 211 Centre Street, on 617-698-0200.

The town describes the cemetery as holding some of the best examples of funerary art from colonial times through the Victorian era, and calls its seventeenth- and eighteenth-century monumentation an artistic and historic record of the town's heritage. That is not marketing language on a headstone-care site — it is the town's own assessment, and it is the right frame for how these stones should be treated.

Access hours are layered. Grounds are open every day 8:00 a.m. until dark. All gates are open during office hours — Monday to Thursday 8:30 to 3:30, Friday 8:30 to 1:30 — and the side gate nearest the office stays open seven days a week from 8:00 a.m. to sunset. The cemetery may close in high winds or for snow removal, so call ahead in bad weather.

Three cemeteries in one

Understanding the layout makes everything else easier, because the three sections are effectively different cemeteries with different stone and different problems.

The original Common Burial Ground occupies about three acres at the northern edge along Centre Street, and was in use from 1672 to 1854. It is laid out linearly, in the colonial manner, and holds a large number of early slate tablets.

The "New Cemetery" came from 60-plus acres acquired between 1854 and 1888. The Trustees hired several noted landscape gardeners to lay it out on rural-cemetery principles — the firms of Whitman and Breck, Copeland and Cleveland, and Ernest Bowditch all contributed plans. The result is winding streets, rolling terrain, a man-made pond, native trees and perennials, and fieldstone wall boundaries.

A modern section was added in 1945.

It is also an accredited arboretum

Milton Cemetery holds Level II arboretum accreditation from ArbNet, and is listed as Milton Cemetery and Arboretum. The collection runs to old New England natives — sugar maples, oaks, tulip trees, lindens — alongside later additions such as yellowwood, sourwood, and dawn redwood, plus the spring flowering trees the Boston area is known for. Nineteenth-century benefactors funded plantings of elms and evergreens, and the property was shaped by the naturalistic design philosophy associated with Frederick Law Olmsted.

That canopy is lovely and it is also the single biggest factor in how stones weather here. More on that below.

Who is buried here

  • Wendell Phillips, the abolitionist and orator, and an advocate for Native American rights.
  • Howard Deering Johnson, founder of the Howard Johnson's restaurant and hotel company.
  • Dennis Miller Bunker, the American Impressionist painter.
  • James A. Burke, United States Representative from Massachusetts.
  • Elbie Fletcher, Major League Baseball player.
  • Medal of Honor recipients Edward A. Gisburne and Paul H. Weinert.

Plantings and decoration rules

The Cemetery Department does not publish a rules and regulations document that we have been able to find, and we are not going to invent one — particularly at a National Register cemetery where the Trustees have good reason to be protective about what goes near the early stones.

Call the department at 617-698-0200 before planting or placing anything. Telephone inquiries are what the department itself encourages. If you learn the current rules, we would be glad to hear them and will add them here with a note on when they were confirmed.

For a sense of how much this varies, the cemeteries in this section run from First Parish in Norwell, which permits permanent beds and shrubs, to Hingham Cemetery, where fresh flowers last a week and nothing artificial is allowed at all. Mayflower in Duxbury, the other town-run cemetery we have written up, permits nothing whatsoever around a flush marker. Do not assume Milton follows any of them. General guidance is in what to plant at a grave site.

Stone care here

Milton is inland, up against the Blue Hills, so salt spray is not the issue it is on the coast. The issue here is the canopy. An accredited arboretum with mature sugar maples, oaks, and lindens produces exactly the conditions that biological growth wants: shade through most of the day, slow drying after rain, and a steady fall of leaf litter and organic debris. Algae, moss, and lichen establish faster under a good tree than almost anywhere else, and north-facing stones under canopy are the worst case of all. Coastal and shaded stone care covers why a shaded inland plot often wants the same 12-to-18-month attention as an exposed coastal one.

The material range here is as wide as anywhere we work. The Common Burial Ground holds colonial slate tablets from the earliest period of English settlement in Massachusetts — genuinely fragile, genuinely irreplaceable, and the last stones on earth that should meet a wire brush or a bottle of bleach. The Victorian sections bring marble, which is soft and etches under acid. The 1945 section and later is largely granite. Granite, marble, and slate covers how each behaves.

We clean with D/2 Biological Solution over a minimum of three visits — no pressure washing, no wire brushes, nothing that leaves salts in the stone. Why D/2 and not bleach sets out what household cleaners actually do to porous stone, and on a 1690s slate tablet the argument stops being theoretical.

We have not found a published rule at Milton restricting who may clean a stone, as some cemeteries have. We would still speak to the department before working on the grounds, which at a National Register cemetery is more than courtesy.

Finding a burial here

A map of the cemetery streets is posted at the entrances and on the department's site, and the town runs a GIS mapper covering the property. As a municipal cemetery, the department holds the records — call during office hours, or call ahead if you are visiting outside them and need directions to a gravesite.

For an early Milton ancestor, the Common Burial Ground at the northern edge along Centre Street is where to look; it covers 1672 to 1854, and its linear colonial layout is quite different from the winding Victorian sections. Find a Grave and BillionGraves both index the cemetery. How to find an ancestor's grave on the South Shore covers the wider search.

If you have located a grave and cannot get to Milton, we can walk the section, find the stone, and photograph it for a one-time fee of $15–$25. On a weathered slate that reads as blank in flat light, a photograph taken in raking light is often the difference between an illegible stone and a readable one.

Contacting the Cemetery Department

The Milton Cemetery Department is at 211 Centre Street, Milton, MA 02186, on 617-698-0200, open Monday to Thursday 8:30–3:30 and Friday 8:30–1:30, or by appointment. Lot purchases, burials, grave locations, memorial planning, and questions about the rules all go through the department. The town also maintains a Milton Cemetery Gift Fund for donations toward beautification and special projects. Department pages are on the Town of Milton website. We are not affiliated with the town or the Trustees; we are a private care service that works in the cemetery.
